# Why does my espresso taste bitter?

If you have ever pulled a shot at home and made a face at the taste, you are in good company. Home espresso is fussy, and a lot of things can push a shot from rich and sweet to harsh and bitter. The good news is that most of the causes are things you can see and change once you know where to look, and you do not need new gear to start.

## What is extraction?

Espresso is made by pushing hot water at about 9 bar of pressure through a tight puck of roughly 18 grams of finely ground coffee. As the water moves through, it dissolves flavors out of the grounds in a set order:
1. acids and fruity notes come out first
2. sugars follow
3. the heavier, woody, bitter compounds come out last

A good shot stops somewhere in the sweet middle of that process. Coffee people call the whole thing extraction, and they talk about shots being under or over extracted.

Extraction is the main idea to keep in your head when a shot tastes wrong:
- a shot that tastes sour and thin is under extracted, because not enough came out
- a shot that tastes bitter, dry, or ashy is over extracted, because too much came out or the wrong things came out too fast

Almost every fix below is a way of nudging extraction back toward the middle.

## Why espresso turns bitter?

"Sour means you stopped too early, bitter means you went too far," says Mira Holt, head trainer at Example Coffee Roasters, who repeats the line to every new barista. The rule is not the whole story, but it is a good place to start. Bitterness usually comes from over extraction, from water that is too hot, or from beans that were roasted very dark.

Bitterness can also come from channeling. Channeling happens when the water finds a weak spot in the puck and rushes through it. The coffee along that path gets over extracted while the rest of the puck barely gets touched, so the cup tastes bitter and sour at the same time. A few habits make channeling more likely:
- uneven tamping
- clumpy grounds
- a puck that is not level

## Does grind size make espresso bitter?

Grind size is the biggest lever you have. A finer grind gives the water more surface to work on and slows the shot down, so more gets extracted. A coarser grind does the opposite. If your shots are bitter and they run slowly, try going a little coarser and see how the next shot tastes.

Grinder drift is where a lot of people get stuck, because a setting that worked last week might be too fine today. Several things change how a grinder behaves:
- fresh beans versus old beans
- a humid day
- the amount of coffee left in the hopper

Most home baristas aim for a shot that takes somewhere around twenty five to thirty seconds, but taste matters more than the clock.

## What water temperature should I use?

Water that is too hot extracts too fast, so dropping the temperature a couple of degrees often takes the edge off a bitter shot. If your machine lets you set the temperature, try that first, especially with darker roasts. Lighter roasts usually want hotter water, and darker roasts usually want cooler water, so there is no single right number for everyone.

If your machine does not have a temperature setting, you still have options. On many single boiler machines the water gets hotter the longer the machine sits idle, so running a little water through the group head before you pull a shot can bring the temperature down a bit.

## Can the beans make espresso bitter?

Sometimes the problem is not your technique at all. Very dark roasts taste bitter almost no matter what you do, because the roasting itself creates a lot of those bitter compounds. Beans that are stale can taste flat and bitter too. On the other hand, beans that were roasted only a day or two ago can be gassy and hard to dial in.

Buying beans with a roast date on the bag, and using them within a few weeks, avoids most of these problems. If you like a smoother cup, a medium roast is usually easier to work with compared to a very dark one, since it gives you more room before the bitterness takes over.

| Roast  | Water temperature   | How bitter it tends to taste  |
| ------ | ------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Light  | Hotter              | Rarely bitter, can taste sour |
| Medium | Middle of the range | Easiest to balance            |
| Dark   | Cooler              | Bitter even when brewed well  |

## What else can I try?

There are a few smaller things worth checking:
- weigh your dose so it is the same every time
- tamp evenly and level
- clean the basket and the group head so old oils do not build up
- try a slightly shorter shot by stopping it a few seconds earlier

Dirty equipment is an underrated cause of bitter coffee, since old coffee oils go rancid and end up in every shot.

## What is the quickest way to fix bitter espresso?

Bitter espresso is frustrating, but it is fixable:
1. change one thing at a time
2. taste every shot
3. keep a few notes so you know what you changed

Most people find that grind size and temperature solve the problem, and the rest is small adjustments.
