# Why does my espresso taste bitter?

If you have ever pulled a shot at home, tasted it, and made a face, you are in good company. Home espresso is fussy, and a lot of things can push a shot from rich and sweet to harsh and bitter. The good news is that most of the causes are things you can see and change once you know where to look, and you do not need new gear to start.

## A little background on extraction

Espresso is made by pushing hot water through a tight puck of finely ground coffee. As the water moves through, it dissolves flavors out of the grounds. The first things to come out are acids and fruity notes, then sugars, and last of all the heavier, woody, bitter compounds. A good shot stops somewhere in the sweet middle of that process. Coffee people call the whole thing extraction, and they talk about shots being under or over extracted.

This is the main idea to keep in your head. When a shot tastes sour and thin, not enough came out. When it tastes bitter, dry, or ashy, too much came out, or the wrong things came out too fast. Almost every fix below is a way of nudging extraction back toward the middle.

## Why espresso turns bitter

There is a line you hear in almost every coffee forum: "Sour means you stopped too early, bitter means you went too far." It is not the whole story, but it is a good place to start. Bitterness usually comes from over extraction, from water that is too hot, or from beans that were roasted very dark.

This can also come from channeling. Channeling happens when the water finds a weak spot in the puck and rushes through it. The coffee along that path gets over extracted while the rest of the puck barely gets touched, so the cup tastes bitter and sour at the same time. Uneven tamping, clumpy grounds, and a puck that is not level all make channeling more likely.

## Grind size

Grind size is the biggest lever you have. A finer grind gives the water more surface to work on and slows the shot down, so more gets extracted. A coarser grind does the opposite. If your shots are bitter and they run slowly, try going a little coarser and see how the next shot tastes.

This is also where a lot of people get stuck, because grinders drift. Fresh beans, old beans, a humid day, and even the amount of coffee left in the hopper can change how your grinder behaves, so a setting that worked last week might be too fine today. Most home baristas aim for a shot that takes somewhere around twenty five to thirty seconds, but taste matters more than the clock.

## Water temperature

Hotter water extracts faster. If your machine lets you set the temperature, dropping it a couple of degrees can take the edge off a harsh shot, especially with darker roasts. Lighter roasts usually want hotter water, and darker roasts usually want cooler water, so there is no single right number for everyone.

If your machine does not have a temperature setting, you still have options. On many single boiler machines the water gets hotter the longer the machine sits idle, so running a little water through the group head before you pull a shot can bring the temperature down a bit.

## The beans themselves

Sometimes the problem is not your technique at all. Very dark roasts taste bitter almost no matter what you do, because the roasting itself creates a lot of those bitter compounds. Beans that are stale can taste flat and bitter too. On the other hand, beans that were roasted only a day or two ago can be gassy and hard to dial in.

This is why it helps to buy beans with a roast date on the bag and to use them within a few weeks. If you like a smoother cup, a medium roast is usually easier to work with compared to a very dark one, since it gives you more room before the bitterness takes over.

## Other things to try

There are a few smaller things worth checking. You can weigh your dose so it is the same every time, you can tamp evenly and level, you can clean the basket and the group head so old oils do not build up, and you can try a slightly shorter shot by stopping it a few seconds earlier. Dirty equipment is an underrated cause of bitter coffee, since old coffee oils go rancid and end up in every shot.

## Final thoughts

Bitter espresso is frustrating, but it is fixable. Change one thing at a time, taste every shot, and keep a few notes so you know what you changed. Most people find that grind size and temperature solve the problem, and the rest is small adjustments.
